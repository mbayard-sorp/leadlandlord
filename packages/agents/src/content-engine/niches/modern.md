# Modern-Tech Overlay (theme=modern)

This overlay tunes the content engine for tech-leaning service businesses
where the install requires technical literacy, certifications, and a
forward-looking pitch. The customer here is researching, comparing specs,
and reading manufacturer pages before they call. Niches in scope: solar
install, EV charger install, smart home, home theater installation,
security systems, network and wifi installs, home automation, surveillance,
fiber and structured wiring, TV wall mount.

These customers buy on competence as much as price. They want the installer
who can answer the third follow-up question without flinching.

## Seasonal Patterns

These niches have softer seasonality than trades, but the buying motions
are real and worth naming.

- Solar: Q1 and Q2 are strong as households finalize tax-year planning
  and target installation before mid-year. The federal ITC step-down
  cycle drives end-of-year urgency in some years; reference deadlines
  generically rather than locking in a year. Spring permitting moves
  quickly; summer interconnection queues lengthen.
- EV charger install: peak summer (vehicle delivery cycles, road-trip
  prep) and again November–December (year-end vehicle purchases, gift
  installs). Tax-credit timing on the home-charger 30C credit drives
  Q4 demand.
- Smart home / automation: Q4 holiday season for retrofit gifting
  (Lutron, video doorbells, smart locks); spring remodel cycle for
  whole-house projects.
- Security / surveillance: spring and fall before vacation seasons;
  uptick after local break-in news cycles.
- Home theater: Q4 (Black Friday TVs, holiday cocooning) and Super
  Bowl run-up January.
- Network / wifi: tied to remodels, new-home moves, work-from-home
  upgrades, and new-build close-outs. Less seasonal, more event-driven.
- Fiber install: follows the local carrier rollout schedule — opportunistic
  on neighborhood activations.

## Regulations to Reference Generically

Signal awareness; do not cite section numbers unless the cluster demands
it.

- NEC (National Electrical Code) — referenced as "current NEC, adopted
  by your AHJ", since adoption lags state by state.
- AHJ (Authority Having Jurisdiction) for permitting and inspection —
  varies by city / county.
- State electrical license and low-voltage license. Many states require a
  separate "limited energy" or "low-voltage" license for structured
  wiring, security, and AV; some states fold it into the electrical
  license. Reference generically.
- EVITP (Electric Vehicle Infrastructure Training Program) certification
  for EV charger installers — increasingly required by automakers and
  utilities for rebate programs.
- NABCEP (North American Board of Certified Energy Practitioners) for
  solar — the gold-standard installer credential.
- UL listing on equipment; UL 2231 for EV charger personnel-protection;
  UL 1741-SA / 1741-SB for solar inverter grid-support.
- Utility interconnection agreements for solar — every install requires
  a signed agreement and PTO before energizing.
- Local rebate and incentive programs — DSIRE database is the source of
  truth; reference programs generically rather than listing specific
  dollar amounts that may change.
- IECC energy-code requirements for new-construction wiring (varies by
  state).
- HIPAA / GDPR considerations for cameras pointed at common spaces are
  out of scope but worth naming if the customer asks about
  multi-tenant installs.

## Common Customer Pain Points

- Solar: "is my roof good for solar?", panel oversizing or undersizing
  vs actual usage, shade from neighbor's tree, HOA approval, breaker
  panel out of capacity (200A vs 100A), production not matching the
  proposal.
- EV: "do I need a panel upgrade?", load calculation surprises, breaker
  capacity, charger placement vs vehicle inlet side, weather
  protection, installer who doesn't pull permits, stub-out vs
  hardwire.
- Network / wifi: dead zones in upstairs bedroom, "we have gigabit but
  the speed test shows 80", mesh vs wired backhaul confusion, ISP
  router vs personal router, Cat5e vs Cat6 in the walls.
- Smart home: too many apps, devices that won't talk to each other,
  Matter vs not-Matter confusion, what happens when wifi goes down,
  what happens when an integrator goes out of business.
- Security: false alarms, monitoring fees, what does "monitoring"
  actually do, can I see footage on my phone, storage retention
  windows.
- Home theater: room acoustics, TV reflections, speaker placement
  fights with the spouse, "my receiver is 8 years old, should I
  upgrade?", HDMI handshake issues.
- Permit lag: "you said two weeks and we're at six". Set expectations
  early.
- Warranty stack: who covers what — manufacturer, installer,
  workmanship.

## Common Objections to Address

1. **"What if I move?"** — Solar transfers with the home and is shown to
   add resale value (cite generic studies, not specific dollars). EV
   chargers either stay (sell with house) or get re-installed at the
   new place. Smart home — most components transfer; bring the hub.
2. **"Isn't this just a fad?"** — Solar is utility-scale infrastructure
   with 25+ year warranties on panels. EV is now mainstream — every
   major automaker is committed. Smart home is moving toward open
   standards (Matter) so device lock-in is decreasing.
3. **"What's your warranty actually cover?"** — Be specific: workmanship
   (typically 5–10 years), manufacturer parts (panels 25 years,
   inverters 10–25, batteries 10), and labor on warranty replacements.
   Spell out what's NOT covered (squirrel damage, lightning, customer
   modifications).
4. **"Will it work if the power goes out?"** — Grid-tie solar without
   battery: no. With battery + protected loads panel: yes, on
   essentials.
5. **"Will my panel handle the load?"** — Honest load calculation
   per NEC 220. If panel upgrade is needed, it's a separate line
   item, not a surprise on day-of.
6. **"Why is your quote higher than the door-knocker?"** — Permit pull,
   licensed master on the install, EVITP / NABCEP-certified, not a
   sub-of-a-sub. Equipment tier matters too — there's a real gap
   between Tier 1 panels and bargain bin.
7. **"How long does the install take?"** — Solar: 1–2 days on the
   roof, then 2–8 weeks for utility PTO. EV charger: 4–8 hours.
   Whole-home network: 1–3 days. Be honest about the wait windows.
8. **"What about cybersecurity?"** — Default-deny configs, segmented
   IoT VLAN, password rotation, no UPnP, firmware update plan.
9. **"What if a panel fails in year 12?"** — Manufacturer warranty
   handles parts; workmanship warranty covers labor for the
   diagnosis and swap. Show the paperwork up front.
10. **"Can you integrate with what I already have?"** — Audit the
    existing kit on a site visit before quoting; do not promise blind.

## Tone Notes

Modern-tech voice is professional, future-oriented, and quietly confident
in technical depth. Write to a customer who has already read three
manufacturer spec sheets — respect their research. Use the right term the
first time and define it briefly if a non-expert page demands it. Avoid
hype words ("revolutionary", "game-changing") — show competence through
specificity instead. Frame installs around outcomes (kWh produced, miles
of EV range per hour of charging, square feet of coverage) rather than
features. Sound like the engineer in the room, not the salesperson at the
booth.
