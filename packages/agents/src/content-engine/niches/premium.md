# Premium-Craft Overlay (theme=premium)

This overlay tunes the content engine for high-end design-and-build
service businesses where the project is a months-long investment in the
home and the customer is choosing on craftsmanship, references, and
process — not on price. Niches in scope: custom landscape design,
swimming pool builders, kitchen remodel, bathroom remodel, custom
cabinetry, hardwood flooring (custom installs and refinishing),
Venetian plaster and specialty finishes, custom millwork, architectural
metalwork, home additions.

The customer here has typically already shopped two or three
competitors, has a Pinterest board, knows their architect or designer,
and is looking for the firm that will hold the schedule, hold the
finish, and not surprise them on cost.

## Terminology

Use industry vocabulary the way a project manager or GC would —
specific, accurate, and not over-explained.

- Process and contracts: "design-build", "design-bid-build",
  "preconstruction", "feasibility study", "scope of work",
  "specifications book", "AIA contracts" (A101 / A102 / A201),
  "fixed price vs cost-plus vs GMP (guaranteed maximum price)",
  "allowances", "change order", "submittal", "RFI (request for
  information)", "punch list", "substantial completion", "final
  walkthrough", "lien waiver", "retainage".
- Site and structural: "tear-out and prep", "demo", "subgrade",
  "subfloor", "plywood underlayment", "moisture mitigation",
  "vapor barrier", "structural engineer's letter", "load-bearing
  wall removal", "LVL beam", "steel beam", "framing inspection",
  "rough-in", "trim-out", "finish-out".
- Cabinetry / millwork: "framed vs frameless", "inset doors",
  "full overlay", "partial overlay", "shaker", "Slab", "five-piece
  door", "soft-close hinges", "undermount drawer slides", "dovetail
  drawer box", "AWI Premium grade", "stile and rail",
  "face-frame", "scribe", "filler strips".
- Flooring: "site-finished vs prefinished", "engineered vs solid",
  "wide plank", "rift and quartered", "live sawn", "cupping",
  "crowning", "moisture content", "acclimation", "screen and
  recoat", "dustless refinishing", "water-based vs oil-based

- Pool and landscape: "shotcrete vs gunite", "rebar cage",

## Seasonal Patterns

Premium projects schedule around the customer's life calendar more than
weather, but there are real cycles.

- Pool builds: peak shovels-in-ground October through January in warm
  climates — winter starts so the pool is open for spring and summer.
  Permitting in summer for fall starts. Avoid promising openings in
  the high season.
- Kitchen remodels: starts cluster January through March (post-holiday,
  before summer travel) and again September (back-to-school
  reset). Avoid late-November starts — finish-out collides with
  holidays.
- Bathroom remodels: similar cadence, plus opportunistic spring
  starts when the homeowner is doing exterior projects in parallel.
- Home additions and custom millwork: 6–12 month projects scoped in
  Q4 / Q1, breaking ground in spring, dried-in by summer, finished
  by year-end.
- Hardwood refinishing: peak summer (windows open, fast cure) and
  pre-listing real-estate cycles.
- Landscape design: design and contract in winter, install in
  spring through early summer; planting windows tightly weather-
  driven.
- Specialty finishes (Venetian plaster, etc.): year-round in
  controlled interiors.

## Regulations to Reference Generically

- State General Contractor license (GC), often with monetary thresholds
  ($30K, $50K, varies). Reference: "fully licensed [STATE] general
  contractor".
- AHJ permitting and inspection — pre-construction, framing,
  electrical, plumbing, mechanical, insulation, drywall, final.
- EPA RRP (Renovation, Repair, and Painting) lead-safe certification
  for any work in pre-1978 homes.
- ANSI / APSP-7 (now ICC/ANSI) standards for residential pool
  barriers, suction-entrapment safety (Virginia Graeme Baker Act).
- Local pool fence and self-closing-gate requirements; alarms on
  doors leading to pool area.
- AWI (Architectural Woodwork Institute) Premium grade for custom
  millwork specs.
- NWFA (National Wood Flooring Association) installer certification.
- Energy code (IECC) requirements on additions — insulation, glazing,
  air sealing, blower-door testing in some jurisdictions.
- AIA contract familiarity — many architect-led projects come with
  AIA forms; reference fluency.
- Lien law — preliminary notices, lien waivers per state.
- Workers' comp and general liability — reference policy limits
  on quote.

## Common Customer Pain Points

- Scope creep: the kitchen project that was "just cabinets and
  counters" growing into wall removal, electrical, HVAC relocate,
  and a new floor.
- Change orders: surprise costs and delays mid-project, especially
  in remodels of older homes (knob-and-tube wiring, asbestos floor
  tile, undersized framing, no header).
- Finish-out timing: appliances, plumbing fixtures, lighting all
  arriving on different schedules; long-lead specialty items
  (custom cabinetry 12–18 weeks, certain stone, imported tile).
- Living through construction: dust, noise, kitchen-less weeks,
  contractor in the house at 7 AM, dogs and toddlers.
- Coordination across trades: who's running point, who's the
  decision-maker, what happens when the electrician needs an answer
  and the homeowner is on a flight.
- Designer-builder friction: scope handoff, who owns the schedule,
  who owns the budget.
- Communication cadence: weekly meetings, written meeting notes,
  shared decision logs.
- Trust-but-verify on the bid: is this fixed-price or full of
  allowances waiting to balloon?
- Substantial-completion vs final-completion: what counts as "done",
  what's on the punch list.
- Warranty after the contract closes: who answers the phone in
  year 2 when a cabinet door starts to sag.

## Common Objections to Address

1. **"Can you match my existing fixtures?"** — Yes when the line is
   still produced; honest "no, here are equivalent options" when not.
   Show samples and mockups before ordering.
2. **"What's your worst project look like?"** — Lean into it. Show
   a project that ran long or had a change-order story, and how it
   was handled. Customers trust contractors who don't pretend
   nothing has ever gone wrong.
3. **"I got a quote for half this."** — Walk through what's in the
   number: licensed and insured, written specifications, allowance
   transparency, warranty, project management hours, the people
   actually in the home (employees vs day-labor subs).
4. **"How long will this take?"** — Realistic: kitchen 8–14 weeks
   construction, longer if cabinetry is custom; primary bath 6–10
   weeks; pool 3–6 months; addition 6–12 months. Build in buffer.
5. **"What's your change-order policy?"** — Written, signed, dollar
   amount and schedule impact named before work proceeds. No
   verbal change orders.
6. **"Do you have references in my neighborhood?"** — Yes; offer a
   tour of a recent comparable project.
7. **"Who's the project manager and how often will I hear from
   them?"** — Named PM, weekly site meetings, daily progress
   email or app updates.
8. **"What about clean-up at end of day?"** — Broom-clean daily,
   site protection (Ram Board, plastic dust walls, HEPA filters
   if needed), end-of-project deep clean.
9. **"What happens if I want to change something mid-build?"** —
   Honest: cost and time impact scale with how late the change
   comes. Cabinet color the day after order ≠ cabinet color the
   day after delivery.
10. **"What's covered after we're done?"** — Workmanship warranty
    (typically 1–2 years on labor, longer on structural),
    manufacturer warranties pass-through, year-one walkthrough.

## Tone Notes

Premium-craft voice is understated, deliberate, and confident in
craft without bragging about it. Write the way a senior project
manager speaks at a kickoff meeting: clear, paced, comfortable with
silence. Show specificity (radius corner detail, reveal dimensions,
finish schedule) rather than adjectives ("luxury", "elegant",
"stunning") — let the work speak. Acknowledge cost plainly and tie
it to durable value (15-year cabinetry, lifetime structural,
finish that holds up to kids and dogs). Avoid hard-sell language;
this customer chooses on trust built across many small touches.
