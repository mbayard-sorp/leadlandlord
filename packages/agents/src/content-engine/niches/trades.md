# Trade-Classic Overlay (theme=classic)

This overlay tunes the content engine for blue-collar service trades that win
on trust, speed, and plain-spoken expertise. Apply this voice to homes, repair,
exterior maintenance, and routine seasonal work. Niches in scope include:
tree removal, junk removal, gutter cleaning, pressure washing, lawn care, pest
control, HVAC, plumbing, electrical, roofing, fencing, concrete, masonry,
painting, drywall, carpentry, handyman, septic, well pumps, and garage doors.

The customer here is usually a homeowner with a real problem to solve right
now — a clog, a leak, a stain, a critter, a sagging gutter — and they want
someone reliable to show up, do the job, and leave the place better than they
found it. Speak to that.

## Terminology

Use these terms naturally where they fit. Do not pad — write like a foreman,
not a thesaurus.

- General trade vocabulary: "service call", "trip charge", "haul-off fee",
  "diagnostic fee", "punch list", "scope of work", "free quote", "free
  estimate", "after-hours", "emergency call", "same-day service", "next-day
  service", "licensed and insured", "bonded".
- Gutter and roof: "gutter guards", "downspout extension", "fascia board",
  "soffit", "drip edge", "valley", "underlayment", "ice dam", "ridge vent",

## Seasonal Patterns

Reference the cycle homeowners already feel — the content reads truer when
the timing matches what's actually happening in their yard.

- Gutter cleaning peaks October–November (leaves down) and again April
  (pollen and oak tassels). Mention "before the first freeze" or "after
  the last big leaf drop".
- Roofing: storm-driven year-round, but inspection-driven spring and fall.
  Hailstorm-belt regions (TX, OK, CO, MO, NE) see Q2 spikes.
- HVAC: pre-summer tune-ups March–May ("don't wait for the first 95-degree
  day"); pre-winter heating service September–October. Emergency demand
  follows the first heat wave and first cold snap.
- Plumbing: water heater failures peak January (cold inlet water on aging
  tanks). Frozen-pipe calls in single-digit weeks. Yard line breaks in
  spring thaw.
- Pest control: spring activation (March–May) for ants, spiders, wasps;
  rodent exclusion September–November as they move indoors; mosquito and
  flea programs May–September.
- Lawn care: mowing March–November in most US, year-round in deep south.
  Aeration / overseeding fall (cool-season) or spring (warm-season).
- Concrete / masonry / fencing: dry-season work — avoid hard freezes and
  prolonged rain. May–October sweet spot in most regions.
- Pressure washing: spring "wake the house up" and pre-listing real estate
  cleans year-round.
- Tree removal: dormant pruning December–February; storm-damage removal
  follows weather events.
- Septic / well: pump-outs every 3–5 years on a rolling schedule; failures
  spike during heavy rain and during holidays (full house = full tank).
- Garage doors: spring failures (broken springs from cold-cycle stress).

## Regulations to Reference Generically

Mention the existence of these in passing — never give legal advice. Build
trust by signaling awareness, not by quoting code sections.

- State contractor license numbers — "licensed in [STATE]", "license
  number on every truck and quote".
- EPA Section 608 refrigerant certification for HVAC technicians handling
  refrigerant.
- EPA RRP (Renovation, Repair, and Painting) lead-safe certification for
  homes built before 1978 — drywall, painting, window replacement.
- OSHA fall-protection standards for roofing crews ("we're tied off,
  always").
- Local backflow prevention testing certifications for plumbing (varies
  by AHJ — Authority Having Jurisdiction).
- State pest control applicator license, often with category endorsements
  (general, termite, wood-destroying organisms).
- State electrical license (master / journeyman) and permit-pull
  authority.
- ICC / ICC-certified inspectors referenced for roofing or framing.
- Tree care: ISA-certified arborist, TCIA-accredited company.
- Septic: state on-site wastewater installer / pumper license.
- Well: state water-well contractor license; potability testing per state
  health department.
- Garage doors: IDEA-certified installers (less universal — only mention
  if relevant).

When in doubt, default to: "licensed, bonded, and insured in
[STATE-ABBREVIATION]". This is the trust phrase customers actually scan for.

## Common Customer Pain Points

The pages should name the pain before pitching the fix. Real customer
worries in this segment:

- Water where it shouldn't be: clogged downspouts dumping next to the
  foundation, slab leaks, sewer backups, basement flooding, ice dams.
- Things that won't turn on: AC that quit during a heat wave, furnace that
  won't ignite, water heater showing a cold shower, garage door dead with
  the car trapped inside.
- Storm damage: missing shingles, downed limbs on the roof or fence,
  tree leaning toward the house.
- Things crawling in: ants in the kitchen, wasps under the eaves, mice in
  the attic, termites in the framing, roach problem the renters left.
- Curb appeal under pressure: pre-listing clean for a real-estate sale,
  HOA letter about the fence or the driveway, party in two weeks.
- Safety: pet and family safety around chemicals, kids and electrical
  panels, the older parent's bathroom that needs grab-bars.
- Emergency timing: water shooting out of a wall at 11 PM, septic alarm
  going off, no heat with a baby in the house.
- Repair vs replace: "is this water heater worth fixing or do I just
  replace it?", "patch the roof or tear off?".
- Disruption: how long will my driveway be unusable, will the yard be a
  mess, do I have to be home, do you wear shoe covers.
- Pricing surprise: hidden fees, change orders, the $89 service call that
  turned into $1,400.

## Common Objections to Address

Bake these into FAQ content, service-page bodies, and meta-descriptions —
the customer is already saying these in their head as they read.

1. **"The price is too high."** — Lead with itemization: parts, labor,
   warranty, haul-off, permit, cleanup. Compare to the cost of redoing
   it cheap. Mention financing if offered.
2. **"Can my brother-in-law just do it?"** — Frame around safety, code,
   insurance, warranty. "We pull the permit, we sign the inspection,
   you don't lose the warranty if a covered part fails."
3. **"Is your guy insured?"** — Yes, both general liability and workers'
   comp. Certificates available on request, included on quote.
4. **"How fast can you get here?"** — Concrete commitments where
   possible: same-day, next-day, 2-hour window. Emergency line is
   answered by a person, not a machine.
5. **"Will you be the cheapest quote?"** — No, and here's why: licensed,
   insured, warrantied, single point of contact, no subs we haven't
   trained.
6. **"What's your warranty?"** — Be specific: 1-year labor, manufacturer
   parts warranty pass-through, 10-year on roof workmanship, etc.
   Vague warranties = no trust.
7. **"Do I have to be home?"** — Often no for outdoor work. Clear
   communication on access, gate codes, pets.
8. **"Will you make a mess?"** — Drop cloths, shoe covers, broom-clean
   finish, haul-off included.
9. **"What if you damage something?"** — Insured, photographed before/
   after, walk-the-job at completion before payment.
10. **"What if it doesn't fix the problem?"** — We come back. Diagnostic
    fee credited toward the repair.

## Tone Notes

Trades-classic voice is confident, plain-spoken, and refuses fluff. Write
like a foreman who's been on the job 20 years and respects the customer's
time — short sentences, real verbs, no marketing softening. Avoid corporate
hedges ("solutions", "leverage", "best-in-class"); say what you do and what
it costs. Acknowledge the problem before pitching the fix — homeowners want
to feel understood, not sold to. Trust signals (license, insurance,
warranty, response time) belong above the fold. Humor is fine in moderation
when it lands; jokes about the job site, not the customer.
